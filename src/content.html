Part 1  ·  Reading Technique

How to read an IELTS passage, what to mark, and the five steps that apply to every question in the test. Everything after it assumes this.

1. How to read the passage — and how much

The mistake almost everyone makes is reading the passage carefully from start to finish before looking at the questions. You do not have time for that, and it does not help, because you cannot remember which detail sat where.

Do this instead:

StageTimeWhat you do
1. Orientation1–2 minRead the title. Read the first sentence of each paragraph only. You are building a rough map: what is this about, and roughly where is each part of the argument.
2. Read the questions1 minLook at what is being asked before you read closely. Now you know what to hunt for.
3. Targeted readingmost of your timeQuestion by question. Find the place, read that place properly, answer, move on.
4. Check2 minAll boxes filled, spelling, word limits.

You never read the whole passage closely. By the end you will have read most of it, but only the parts the questions sent you to.

2. What to mark on the paper

Marking is not decoration. You are leaving yourself a trail so you do not have to search the same paragraph four times. Mark three things only:

  • Names, numbers, dates and technical terms — circle them. These are the things the questions use to point you at a place, because they are the only words the passage cannot reword.
  • Linking words that turn the argument — but, however, although, yet, in fact, though. Underline them. The meaning of a paragraph very often sits on the far side of one of these.
  • The question number — when you find an answer, write the question number in the margin next to the line. Later questions are usually nearby.

Do not highlight whole sentences. If everything is marked, nothing is marked.

3. The five steps, used on every single question

The procedure
  1. Read the question and decide what kind of claim it makes. Is it about a fact, a quantity, a comparison, a cause, or someone's opinion? This takes two seconds and tells you what to look out for.
  2. Pick the one word you can search for. Not the important idea — that will have been reworded. Pick the name, number, date or unusual noun. That is your pointer.
  3. Read the sentence you land on, plus one before and one after. Never just the line. The detail that changes the answer is very often in the next sentence.
  4. Check every word of the question against the passage. Each word in the question should have something in the passage that matches it. If one word has nothing to match it, that word is the answer to the question — usually it means NOT GIVEN, or it means an option is wrong.
  5. Answer, then check one thing only: the words all, most, some, never, only, may, proves. Do not re-read the whole sentence. That is how people talk themselves out of correct answers.

Step 4 is the one that separates a 7 from an 8. Most people, when the sentence looks nearly right, quietly forgive the one word that does not fit. That word is the question.

4. The passage never uses the same words as the question

This is the whole game. The question is always a reworded version of a passage sentence, and there are only six ways it gets reworded:

TypePassageQuestion
A different wordnumbers declineda fall in numbers
Verb becomes a nounprices rose steadilya steady rise in prices
Active becomes passiveresearchers found the sitethe site was found
Clauses swapped roundAlthough costly, it worked.It worked despite the cost.
Opposite with a negativefew applicants qualifiedmost applicants failed to qualify
Examples become a categoryoak, beech and ashdeciduous trees

The last two catch people out most. A sentence with no negative word in it can still mean something negative — the policy fell short of its target means it failed. And the question can legitimately use a word that appears nowhere in the passage, as long as the passage's examples belong to it.

5. The words that decide the answer

When a question is hard, the difficulty is nearly always one of these words. Learn them as lines, because it is the movement along the line that matters.

How certain:   proves → shows → suggests → may → is unlikely

Bamboo

Bamboo is not a tree but a grass, and it behaves like one. Where an oak spends decades thickening its trunk, a bamboo culm emerges from the ground at close to its final diameter and simply extends upward. Some tropical species lengthen by as much as ninety centimetres in a single day, and a culm reaches its full height within a few months. After that it never grows again. It only hardens, laying down silica in its walls over the following years.

This habit has consequences for anyone harvesting it. A bamboo stand is not felled and replanted but cut selectively, culm by culm, with the youngest left standing. Cutters normally take culms of between three and six years: younger ones are too soft and contain too much starch, while older ones turn brittle. Because the underground rhizome system is untouched, a well-managed stand can be cut every year indefinitely, and this is the origin of bamboo's reputation as a renewable material.

That starch is also the plant's weakness. Untreated bamboo is attractive to powder-post beetles, and in humid conditions a pole may be reduced to dust within two or three years. Traditional practice dealt with this by leaching: culms were submerged in running water or buried in mud for several weeks, which washed the soluble sugars out of them. Modern producers more often use borax solutions, which are cheap and of low toxicity, though they wash out again in exposed positions.

Structurally the material is remarkable. Its tensile strength along the grain exceeds that of mild steel by weight, and the hollow cylinder is an efficient shape in bending. But the anatomy that makes it strong lengthwise makes it weak across the grain. It splits readily, which is why traditional joints tie rather than nail. Engineers have found this the hardest property to design around, and it explains why bamboo has entered modern construction mainly in processed forms — laminated board, strand-woven flooring — rather than as whole poles.

Flowering is the strangest thing about the plant. Most bamboos are monocarpic: they flower once, set seed and die. The interval between flowerings can be extraordinarily long, sixty years in some species and more than a hundred in others, and all plants descended from the same original stock flower simultaneously wherever in the world they happen to be growing. In north-east India the flowering of one species every forty-eight years is followed by a plague of rats, which breed on the abundant seed and then move into the farmers' fields. The event of 2006 was anticipated and prepared for; earlier ones had caused famine.

The economic scale of the plant is easy to underestimate. Something over two billion people live in houses that use bamboo in some form. Yet almost none of this appears in forestry statistics, because bamboo is classified as a non-timber forest product and is often gathered outside any formal system.

The environmental claims made for it are sometimes overstated. A managed stand does capture carbon faster than most timber plantations, and it does so while yielding a crop every year. But much of that advantage disappears if the material is shipped halfway around the world. The honest case for bamboo is narrower than the promotional one. It is an excellent local material, best grown and used within a few hundred kilometres of where it stands.

The rise and fall of the department store

A   The department store was invented twice over, in Paris and in New York, within about a decade of each other in the middle of the nineteenth century. The Parisian version is usually credited to Aristide Boucicaut, who took over a small draper's shop and rebuilt it around three ideas that were then unusual: that goods should carry a fixed price, that the price should be marked on them, and that a customer could come in, look, and leave again without buying anything. Each of these abolished something. Fixed pricing ended haggling, and with it the assumption that the shopkeeper would judge what each customer could afford. Marked prices ended the need to ask. The right to browse ended the obligation that entering a shop had always carried.

B   The third of these mattered far beyond retailing. A respectable woman in 1860 had almost nowhere she could go alone in a city without attracting comment. The department store, by removing the obligation to buy, accidentally created a public interior in which she could spend an afternoon unaccompanied and unremarked. Stores understood what they had done and leaned into it, adding tea rooms, writing rooms, lavatories and eventually crèches. The novelist Émile Zola, who studied Boucicaut's operation closely before writing about it, called the store a machine for selling, but the machine's most durable product may have been social rather than commercial.

C   The buildings were designed to be looked at. Plate glass, which became affordable at precisely the right moment, turned the street frontage into a stage; cast iron allowed interior courts that rose four or five storeys to a glazed roof; electric light, once it arrived, made the interior brighter than the daylight outside. The escalator, introduced for practical reasons, turned out to matter for a different one: unlike a lift, it delivered a continuous stream of customers past merchandise they had not intended to see. None of this was decoration. Every element was there to extend the time a visitor spent inside.

D   Underneath the spectacle the economics were plain and rather fragile. A department store made a low margin on each item and depended entirely on volume, which meant it needed an enormous number of people passing its doors every day. That in turn meant a dense city centre and, above all, public transport. The great stores of the period were almost always built within a few hundred metres of a railway terminus, a tram junction or, later, an underground station. The model worked because the city delivered the crowd; the store did not have to.

E   This is why the car was so damaging. As populations moved outward after 1945, the crowd stopped arriving by tram and started arriving by road, and the city centre that had been the store's greatest asset became its greatest liability: expensive, congested and short of parking. The shopping mall was the direct answer. Developers discovered that they could guarantee footfall by signing a large store as an anchor tenant on favourable terms, then letting the smaller units around it at high rents to retailers who wanted the traffic the anchor generated. Department stores accepted these deals, and in doing so converted themselves from destinations into infrastructure for somebody else's business.

F   The internet finished what the mall had begun, though not in the way that is usually described. Online retailers did not simply undercut the stores; they removed the last advantage the stores had, which was selection. A shop with a hundred thousand square feet can hold a great deal, but it cannot hold everything, and once customers could compare a full range from a phone while standing in the aisle, the store became a showroom for a purchase made elsewhere. Retail analyst Dana Telford has argued that no amount of cost-cutting could have answered this, because the competition was not on price but on range. Marc Levin takes a different view, pointing out that the American chains were carrying two or three times the floor space per customer that their European equivalents were, and that the problem was one of over-expansion which the internet merely exposed.

G   What survives suggests that both may be partly right. The stores that have done best are the ones that stopped trying to sell everything and became something other than shops: buildings people visit for the food hall, the restaurants, the events, with merchandise arranged around the visit rather than the other way round. It is, in a sense, a return to Boucicaut. He too was selling an afternoon rather than a product, and he too understood that the goods were not the reason people came.

Why we forget

A   Forgetting has always been described as a failure. The language gives it away: memory is something we lose, a name escapes us, a fact slips our mind. The research tradition followed the language. When Hermann Ebbinghaus sat down in the 1880s to memorise lists of nonsense syllables and then test himself at intervals, he produced the forgetting curve — a steep initial drop followed by a long shallow tail — and the curve was read, for the next century, as a picture of decay. Something was leaking out of the system, and the interesting question was how to stop it.

B   That reading is now looking doubtful, and the reason is that forgetting turns out to be expensive. Work over the past fifteen years has shown that it is not a passive fading but an active, energy-consuming process with its own machinery. Particular molecules are recruited to dismantle the connections between neurons; block them, and animals retain memories they would normally have shed. A system does not evolve a dedicated mechanism, and then pay to run it, in order to perform worse. If the brain is spending resources to forget, forgetting is doing something for it.

C   What it appears to be doing is keeping memory useful rather than complete. The purpose of remembering is not to archive the past but to act sensibly in the present, and for that purpose a great deal of detail is not merely surplus but harmful. If you park in a different space each morning, the memory that matters is today's, and yesterday's forty locations are interference to be cleared. Generalising works the same way: to recognise a chair you have never seen before, the specific chairs must blur into a category, and blurring is a form of forgetting. On this account, a memory system that retained everything would not be a better memory system. It would be a worse one.

D   The strongest evidence comes from people who approximate that condition. A small number of individuals have what is called highly superior autobiographical memory, and can report, accurately and without effort, what they did and what the weather was like on an arbitrary date decades ago. It is tempting to envy them. But the ability is oddly narrow — it does not extend to learning vocabulary or passing examinations, and on standard memory tests these individuals perform unremarkably. Several describe the experience as intrusive: a chance remark summons a day from thirty years earlier in full and unwanted detail, and old grievances do not fade because the emotional colour comes back with everything else.

E   Sleep is where a good deal of the work is done, and it does two apparently contradictory jobs at once. Memories tagged as significant during the day are replayed and strengthened; at the same time the overall level of synaptic connection, which has risen throughout the waking hours, is scaled back down. Giulio Tononi, whose group has developed this account, describes the effect as improving the signal-to-noise ratio — the important traces are not made louder so much as everything else is made quieter. Whether the two processes are genuinely separate or two descriptions of a single mechanism is still argued about, and the evidence does not yet settle it.

F   None of this means that forgetting is always benign, and the argument is easy to push too far. The mechanisms that clear a car park also clear things we would rather keep, and in the dementias they are not operating in some adaptive fashion but breaking down altogether. The claim is narrower: that in a healthy brain, forgetting is a regulated process which has been selected for, and that treating every instance of it as a malfunction is a mistake about what memory is for.

G   The practical consequence is a change of question. Educational advice has concentrated for a century on how to retain more — on repetition, mnemonics, spaced practice. Very little attention has gone to what a learner should be encouraged to let go, or to the possibility that some of the difficulty people have in acquiring new patterns is the persistence of old ones. That seems to me the more interesting problem, and it has barely been looked at.

PATTERN 1True / False / Not Given
The difference between "the passage says the opposite" and "the passage doesn't say"
Technique

Three answers are possible and each needs a different kind of proof:

  • TRUE — everything in the statement is supported by the passage.
  • FALSE — the passage says something that cannot be true at the same time. You can point at the words.
  • NOT GIVEN — there is a word in the statement that has nothing in the passage to match it.

So the question to ask is never "is this true in real life?" It is: can I point at the words that make this impossible? If you can, FALSE. If the best you can say is "it doesn't really mention that", NOT GIVEN.

Passage — the urban heat island

The temperature gap between a city and the countryside around it is not a uniform effect. It is strongest on still, cloudless nights, when rural land radiates its heat away quickly while brick, asphalt and concrete go on releasing warmth absorbed during the day. On windy nights the gap can vanish almost entirely. Measurements in mid-sized European cities typically show a night-time difference of three to five degrees Celsius. Planners have responded with reflective roofing, street trees, and in a few cities the deliberate reopening of buried rivers.

What you should get from this passage
  1. Two things are given opposite behaviour. Rural land loses heat quickly; concrete and asphalt keep releasing it. Any time a passage does this — property A to one thing, property B to another — expect a question that swaps them round. That swap is a contradiction, even though the question will contain no negative word at all.
  2. "Can vanish almost entirely" is an extreme statement. Note how strong it is. A question will often soften it to something like "is reduced". A softer version of a strong statement is still supported, so that would be TRUE.
  3. Three measures are listed, with no comparison between them. They are joined by commas and "and". There is no more, no most, no better. Expect a question that ranks one above another — and remember that a list is not a ranking.
Q1The heat island effect is reduced when wind speeds are high.
TRUE

Why
The passage says the gap "can vanish almost entirely" on windy nights. Vanishing is the strongest possible form of being reduced, so if it vanishes, it is certainly reduced. The question is a weaker version of what the passage says, and a weaker version of a supported statement is still supported.

The trap here is waiting to see the actual word reduced. It is not there, and it does not need to be.

Q2Rural ground holds its heat for longer after dark than concrete does.
FALSE

Why
The passage says rural land loses heat quickly, and that concrete goes on releasing it. The question says the reverse. The two roles have been swapped.

This is a real contradiction, so the answer is FALSE, not NOT GIVEN — even though every noun in the question appears in the passage and there is no negative word anywhere.

Q3Reflective roofing has proved more effective than planting street trees.
NOT GIVEN

Why
Go through the question word by word. Reflective roofing — in the passage. Street trees — in the passage. More effective than — nothing. The passage names three things planners do. It never says which works best, and it never uses any comparing word.

One word in the question with nothing to match it. That is what NOT GIVEN looks like.

Same pattern next time

A list is not a ranking. When a passage lists things, we naturally assume the first one is the most important. IELTS uses that habit constantly. Unless the passage actually contains more, most, the main, better than or rather than, no ranking exists, and any question claiming one is NOT GIVEN.

A swap is a contradiction. If the passage gives quality A to thing 1 and quality B to thing 2, and the question gives A to thing 2, that is FALSE. Do not let the absence of a negative word talk you into NOT GIVEN.

PATTERN 2True / False / Not Given
When "some" becomes "all" — and why that is FALSE, not NOT GIVEN
Technique

Before you look at the passage, circle any word in the question that says how many or how often: all, every, most, many, some, few, always, never. If one is there, that word is almost certainly the whole question, and the rest of the sentence will match the passage perfectly.

Then find what the passage says on the same scale and compare:

  • Question claims more than the passage → FALSE
  • Question claims less than the passage → TRUE
Passage — seed banks

Seed banks are often described as insurance policies for agriculture. The reality is more modest. Of the collections registered worldwide, the majority hold fewer than ten thousand accessions, and many concentrate on a single crop. Duplication is common: the same widely grown varieties of wheat and rice appear in collection after collection, while the landraces maintained by smallholders are poorly represented. An audit of European collections found that around forty per cent of stored samples had not been regenerated within the recommended interval, raising doubts about their viability.

What you should get from this passage
  1. "The majority hold fewer than ten thousand." Read what this quietly also tells you: a minority hold more than ten thousand. The passage has taken a position on the scale, and that position rules out "all".
  2. "Many concentrate on a single crop." Again a position on the scale — this time a fairly strong one. Anything weaker than "many" is automatically supported.
  3. The European audit sentence is about viability, not coverage. It gives a number (forty per cent), a region (Europe) and a topic (samples not regenerated). A question may use the region and the number to pull you here, then ask about something else entirely. Finding the right-looking sentence is not the same as answering the question.
Q4All registered seed banks hold fewer than ten thousand accessions.
FALSE

Why
The passage says the majority do. "Majority" means most but not all — so the passage is positively telling you that some banks hold ten thousand or more. The question says there are none of those.

That is a genuine clash, so it is FALSE. People often choose NOT GIVEN here because the word "all" is not in the passage. But the passage does not need to contain the word; it only needs to rule it out, and "majority" does.

Q5Some collections are devoted to just one crop.
TRUE

Why
The passage says many do. The question says some. "Some" is a smaller claim than "many", and it is contained inside it — if many do, then certainly some do.

This is the same movement as Q4 but in the opposite direction, which is why the two are placed together. Up the scale is FALSE; down the scale is TRUE.

Q6European collections represent landraces better than collections elsewhere do.
NOT GIVEN

Why
Your eye goes straight to the European sentence, because "European" is there. But read what that sentence is actually about: samples that were not regenerated on time, which is a point about whether the seeds are still alive.

The question is about landrace coverage, compared with other regions. Neither of those appears. The passage does say landraces are poorly represented — but that is a general statement about all collections, not a comparison between Europe and elsewhere.

PATTERN 3True / False / Not Given
Comparisons: the invented one, and the real one that hides
Technique

When a question compares two things, check three things in order:

  1. Are both things in the passage? If not → NOT GIVEN straight away.
  2. Are they measured on the same quality? Two things can be mentioned together without ever being compared.
  3. Is there a comparison signal? Either a comparing word (more, better, exceeds, outlasts) or a contrast structure (while, whereas, unlike) with actual values on both sides.

All three present → the comparison is real; decide TRUE or FALSE. Any one missing → NOT GIVEN.

Passage — Roman concrete

Roman harbour concrete has survived two thousand years of wave action, while modern Portland-cement structures placed in the same waters often show serious decay within fifty years. The reason, identified by researchers analysing cores drilled from ancient breakwaters, is a mineral called aluminous tobermorite, which grows inside the material when seawater percolates through its volcanic ash matrix, knitting the mass together as it goes. Modern concrete is engineered to resist water ingress. The Roman material, in effect, puts it to work.

What you should get from this passage
  1. The first sentence is a comparison, even though it contains no comparing word. There is no "more durable", no "better". But there are two named materials, one shared quality (surviving in seawater), the same conditions ("the same waters"), the word while, and two numbers — two thousand years against fifty. That is a comparison the writer has made, and the numbers tell you which way it goes.
  2. Tobermorite and volcanic ash are next to each other but not compared. The passage says where the mineral grows and what it does. It says nothing about which is harder, stronger or older. Being in the same sentence is not being compared.
  3. "Engineered to resist water ingress" — a clear, simple statement about modern concrete. Note the verb resist. Any question using the opposite verb will be FALSE.
Q7Roman harbour concrete is more durable in seawater than modern Portland-cement concrete.
TRUE

Why
Two thousand years against fifty, in the same waters, joined by while. Both materials, the same quality, and a contrast structure carrying numbers — all three checks pass.

If you have been taught "comparisons are usually NOT GIVEN", this is where that goes wrong. It is a useful warning, not a rule. What matters is whether the comparison is actually being made, not whether a comparing word appears.

Q8Modern concrete is designed to allow seawater to penetrate it.
FALSE

Why
"Modern concrete is engineered to resist water ingress." Resist and allow are opposites, applied to the same thing. Straight contradiction.

Do not be unsettled by the last sentence, which says the Roman material does put water to work. That is the contrast being drawn, and it is about the other material.

Q9Aluminous tobermorite is harder than the volcanic ash surrounding it.
NOT GIVEN

Why
Both are in the passage, and in the same sentence, which makes the question feel checkable. But the quality being asked about — hardness — is never mentioned for either.

It is also quite likely to be true in reality: a mineral that binds a material together probably is harder. That reasoning is not allowed. You answer from the passage, not from what makes sense.

Same pattern next time

Two things being near each other is not a comparison. Check the quality the question names — hardness, cost, speed, importance — and look for that specific quality being measured for both.

But a comparison can exist without a comparing word. While, whereas, unlike, or simply "X did this; Y did that" with numbers attached, all count. Do not answer NOT GIVEN just because there is no "more than".

PATTERN 4True / False / Not Given
"X causes Y" when the passage only says they go together
Technique

Science passages hedge, because that is how research is written: may, suggests, appears to, remains unsettled. Questions then state the same thing as settled fact.

The instinct is to mark that FALSE. It is usually wrong. Ask instead: has the writer closed the door?

  • "we do not know whether X causes Y" → the door is open → NOT GIVEN
  • "trials found no evidence that X causes Y" → the door is closed → FALSE
Passage — sleep and memory

Participants who spent longer in slow-wave sleep on the night after training performed better on a recall test the following morning, and the association held across three separate cohorts. Whether the relationship is causal remains unsettled. Slow-wave sleep may consolidate memory directly; alternatively, both variables may reflect some third factor, such as baseline cognitive fitness. A small trial that used acoustic stimulation to enhance slow-wave activity reported improved recall, but with eighteen participants it was too small to be conclusive.

What you should get from this passage
  1. Sentence 1 reports an association, not a cause. "Participants who spent longer… performed better" means the two things went together. It does not say one produced the other. Mark this distinction — it is one of the most heavily tested things in the whole exam.
  2. "Remains unsettled" means nobody knows. The writer then offers two possible explanations, one of which is direct causation. So the passage is holding the door open for causation, not shutting it.
  3. The third-factor sentence is still live. "May reflect some third factor" is presented as a real possibility under consideration — which means it has definitely not been ruled out.
  4. "Too small to be conclusive" is a plain statement and will be reworded in a question as something like "not definitive".
Q10Spending longer in slow-wave sleep causes better recall the next day.
NOT GIVEN

Why
This feels like it should be FALSE, because the passage explicitly says we do not know whether the relationship is causal. But look at what that actually means. The passage has not said "sleep does not cause better recall". It has said "we have not established whether it does" — and then listed direct causation as one of the live options.

For FALSE you need the passage to make the statement impossible. Here it does the opposite: it leaves room for it. So: unverified, not contradicted. NOT GIVEN.

Q11Researchers have ruled out the possibility that a third factor explains the association.
FALSE

Why
Compare this with Q10 deliberately. That question was about the world (does sleep cause recall?). This one is about what the researchers have done, and the passage is clear: the third factor is offered as a standing alternative explanation.

Something still being offered as a possible explanation has obviously not been ruled out. That is a real clash, so FALSE.

Q12The acoustic stimulation trial was too small for its results to be considered definitive.
TRUE

Why
"Too small to be conclusive" and "too small for its results to be considered definitive" say the same thing. Conclusive and definitive are simply different words for the same idea.

The number eighteen is there to help you find the sentence, not to be the answer.

Same pattern next time

PATTERN 5True / False / Not Given
The condition that arrives in the next sentence
Technique

This is the single most common self-inflicted mistake in the whole test: you find the sentence, it supports the statement, you answer, and the sentence that cancels it was directly underneath.

Two habits fix it:

  • Always read one sentence either side of the one you land on.
  • Break long statements into parts. Anything with if, and, any, even contains more than one claim. Every part must be supported for TRUE. One part contradicted makes the whole thing FALSE, no matter how well the rest matches.
Passage — song learning in sparrows

The white-crowned sparrow acquires its song during a sensitive period in its first summer. Birds reared in isolation produce a crude, unstructured warble. If, however, they hear recordings of adult song during that window — even for only a few minutes a day — they develop a normal adult song the following spring. The recordings must be of their own species; the songs of closely related sparrows are ignored.

What you should get from this passage
  1. Sentence 3 is the one your search will find, because it contains the words about isolation, recordings and normal song. It supports a lot. Read on anyway.
  2. Sentence 4 is the catch, and it is the last sentence. "Must be of their own species" is a condition, and "are ignored" tells you what happens if the condition is not met. Notice that neither clause contains a negative word — the restriction is carried by meaning, not by "not".
  3. "Even for only a few minutes a day" — the word even is doing real work. It means: this small amount is enough. Small words like even, only, just and still change meaning and are easy to skim past.
  4. "First summer" is more specific than "first year". A question using the wider word is still supported, because a summer is inside a year.
Q13Isolated white-crowned sparrows can still develop normal song if they are played brief daily recordings of any sparrow species.

Split the statement into its parts and check each one:

Three out of four parts match beautifully. That is deliberate. One part is contradicted, so the whole statement is FALSE.

Q14Short periods of daily exposure are sufficient for normal song to develop.
TRUE

Why
"Even for only a few minutes a day — they develop a normal adult song." The word even is what makes this about sufficiency: it is telling you that this small amount is already enough.

Q15The sensitive period for song learning occurs within the bird's first year of life.
TRUE

Why
The passage says "its first summer". A summer falls inside a year, so the question is a broader, safer version of what the passage says — the same downward movement you saw in Pattern 2.

Same pattern next time

Watch for these words in a statement: any, all, regardless of, whatever, even, always. They are usually attached to an otherwise accurate sentence, and the sentence that cancels them is close by — often the next one, often short, often after a semicolon.

The rule for multi-part statements: every part supported = TRUE. One part contradicted = FALSE. One part simply missing, none contradicted = NOT GIVEN.

PATTERN 6True / False / Not Given
When "only" does not mean FALSE
Technique

Pattern 2 taught you that extreme words like all and never often signal FALSE. This pattern is the limit of that rule, and it catches good candidates specifically.

The rule only works if the passage has said something on the same scale. Ask: what would this extreme word have to clash with? Then go and look for it.

  • Passage takes a position and the question exceeds it → FALSE
  • Passage says nothing about amount or scope → NOT GIVEN
Passage — polymetallic nodules

Polymetallic nodules lie scattered across abyssal plains at depths of four to six kilometres, built up over millions of years as dissolved metals precipitate around a nucleus — a shark's tooth, a fragment of shell. They are rich in manganese, nickel, cobalt and copper, and commercial interest has sharpened as demand for battery metals has grown. Marine biologists note that the sediment plumes raised by collector vehicles may travel well beyond the mining site, and that the nodule surfaces themselves are habitat for sponges and other slow-growing organisms.

What you should get from this passage
  1. "Four to six kilometres" needs translating, not just locating. A number on its own is not an answer. Turn it into plain words: that is very deep, therefore not shallow, therefore not coastal.
  2. "Precipitate around a nucleus — a shark's tooth, a fragment of shell." Two examples of a solid object. A question may well use the general category ("a solid object") rather than the examples. That is fair rewording, not a missing word.
  3. The last clause says nodules are habitat for sponges. Read exactly what kind of claim that is: it says this habitat exists. It says nothing at all about whether sponges also live somewhere else. That gap is what the next question uses.
Q16Nodules form around a solid object at their centre.
TRUE

Why
"Precipitate around a nucleus — a shark's tooth, a fragment of shell." A tooth and a shell fragment are solid objects, and nucleus means the thing at the centre. Every word of the question is covered.

Q17Polymetallic nodules are found mainly in shallow coastal waters.

Four to six kilometres deep, on abyssal plains. That is the opposite of shallow, and abyssal plains are the opposite of coastal. The clash is on two counts.

So there is nothing for the word only to contradict. The passage does not confirm it and does not deny it. NOT GIVEN.

Technique

Yes/No/Not Given works exactly like True/False/Not Given, with one extra job: it asks about the writer's view. So passages for this type are deliberately full of other people's opinions.

Passage — open-plan offices

The case for the open-plan office was always thinner than its advocates claimed. Proponents such as the designer Elena Roth argued that removing walls would multiply spontaneous encounters and therefore ideas. When researchers finally measured this, using sensors worn by employees, face-to-face interaction fell by around seventy per cent after firms moved to open layouts, while electronic messaging rose. Roth's response — that the measurements captured a transitional period — is not persuasive; follow-up data collected a year later show the same pattern. What open plan reliably delivers is not collaboration but a reduction in floor space per employee, which is, one suspects, the point.

What you should get from this passage
  1. The first sentence tells you the writer's position immediately. "Thinner than its advocates claimed" means the writer does not believe the case. Everything after this is either evidence for that view or a view being knocked down.
  2. "Proponents such as Elena Roth argued that…" — this is the trap sentence. The idea that open plan increases spontaneous encounters belongs to Roth, not to the writer. And because it is quoted almost word for word, a question built on it will match perfectly. In this question type, a perfect word match next to a reporting verb is a warning sign.
  3. "Is not persuasive" — no source attached, so this is the writer speaking directly. This is what an unmistakable writer opinion looks like.
  4. "One suspects" is a hedge, but it is the writer hedging about the writer's own view. That still counts as the writer's opinion. Do not downgrade hedged statements to NOT GIVEN.
  5. "Electronic messaging rose" is a measurement, not a judgement. The writer never says whether messaging is a good or bad way to work.

NO

"Is not persuasive." No source attached, so this is the writer's own judgement, and persuasive and convincing mean the same thing. The writer denies it directly.

Same pattern next time

Watch these verbs. Each one hands the opinion to somebody else: argued, claimed, maintained, insisted, suggested, according to, in the view of, proponents say, critics contend.

Then find the writer's reply. If the source is followed by but, however, in fact, or a phrase like is not persuasive, the writer disagrees. If it is followed by supporting evidence, the writer agrees.

A hedged opinion is still an opinion. One suspects, arguably, it seems, it would be unwise to — these can all support YES or NO.

PATTERN 8Yes / No / Not Given
The agreement that comes before the disagreement
Technique

Good writing usually admits the other side first and then turns: that is true — and yet. Questions are built on the half that was admitted.

So stop at every turning word and ask which side of it your evidence is on: though, yet, however, nevertheless, still, in practice, that said, but.

The material before the turn is being given away. The writer's actual point is after it.

Passage — machine translation

Predictions that machine translation would end the need for human translators have been made, confidently, for six decades. They may finally be approaching the truth in narrow domains: weather bulletins, product listings, patent abstracts. Elsewhere the picture is less tidy. Literary translation demands choices about register and rhythm that no current system makes reliably, and legal translation carries liabilities that firms are unwilling to automate away. It would be unwise, though, for translators to assume the boundary will hold where it is. The set of tasks regarded as safe has narrowed steadily, and there is no principle that fixes its edges.

What you should get from this passage
  1. "May finally be approaching the truth in narrow domains: weather bulletins, product listings, patent abstracts." The hedge is may, but the colon then gives three concrete examples. That combination — a hedge plus specific examples — is a writer committing to a view.
  2. The literary and legal sentence is the concession. It spends two clauses explaining why some translation resists machines. A question built on this half will feel very well supported.
  3. Then comes though. One small word, in the middle of the sentence, and it reverses everything. The writer's real point is: do not assume this will last.
  4. Literary and legal are given different reasons for resisting automation — one about ability, one about risk. They are never placed on a shared scale of difficulty.
  5. "Has narrowed steadily" will be reworded. Expect shrunk, decreased, got smaller over time.

PATTERN 9Matching Headings
The heading that matches the paragraph, not the sentence you remember
Technique

There are always more headings than paragraphs, and the spare ones are not random. Each is built from a real sentence of the passage — usually the most vivid one. So a heading that matches something you clearly remember reading is more suspicious, not less.

  1. Cover the list of headings. Read the paragraph first.
  2. Read the first sentence and the last sentence, then say what the paragraph is about in about six words of your own.
  3. Now look at the list and find the heading that matches your own summary.
  4. Check it: run down the paragraph and ask whether each sentence serves that heading. If you have to ignore most of the paragraph, you have picked a detail.
  5. Do the paragraphs you are sure about first, and cross those headings off.
Passage — the coming of railway time

A   Until the middle of the nineteenth century, every British town kept its own time, set by the sun at local noon. Bristol ran about ten minutes behind London, Plymouth about sixteen. Nobody found this inconvenient, because nothing moved fast enough for the difference to matter: a stagecoach took so long to cross the country that the discrepancy was absorbed by the journey itself. Clocks were local instruments for local life.

B   The railways destroyed that arrangement within a decade. A timetable is a promise about simultaneity, and a network cannot make such promises if each station measures the hour differently. Trains ran on single tracks with passing loops, so a train delayed by a few minutes at one end could meet another head-on. The companies did not wait for permission: from 1840 the Great Western began running its entire system on London time, and the other lines followed.

C   Outside the stations, the new hour was resented. Towns that had kept their own time for centuries saw no reason to be corrected by a private company, and several corporations instructed their churches to ignore it. Exeter cathedral kept local time on one clock face and railway time on another, with two minute hands on a single dial. Disputes reached the courts: in one celebrated case a defendant arrived at ten o'clock by the town's reckoning, found the hearing already closed, and appealed on the ground that the judge had used the wrong ten o'clock. He won.

What you should get from these paragraphs
  1. A: local time varied, and nobody minded, because travel was slow. Two halves — the situation, and why it was fine. A heading needs to cover both.
  2. B: railways needed one clock, so the companies imposed one. The head-on collision sentence is alarming and memorable, but look at its job: it is one clause explaining why simultaneity mattered. And note that no accident is reported as actually happening.
  3. C: the first sentence states the point outright — "the new hour was resented". Everything after is illustration: the churches, the two-handed clock, the court case. Both the clock and the court case are vivid, and both will appear as tempting headings.
  4. The court case is the harder temptation because it takes up a third of the paragraph and comes last. But look at what it shows: the defendant won because nobody could say which ten o'clock was official. It demonstrates confusion, not legal reform.

PATTERN 10Matching Headings
When two headings both seem to fit
Technique

Do not re-read the paragraph a third time. Run four checks in order, and stop at the first one that separates them. This takes fifteen seconds instead of ninety.

  1. Coverage — which heading accounts for more of the sentences?
  2. Accuracy — does either heading claim something the paragraph never actually says? A wrong claim kills a heading regardless of scope.
  3. Job — is the paragraph describing, explaining, arguing or recommending? Match the heading to that.
  4. Elsewhere — would the rejected heading fit another paragraph better? Then let it go.
Passage — the coffee trade

D   Of the price paid for a cup of coffee in a European city, the grower who produced the beans receives something close to one per cent. The figure is often quoted as evidence of exploitation, and it is certainly evidence of something, but the arithmetic is less mysterious than it looks. Almost everything that determines what a customer will pay — roasting, blending, branding, the rent on the premises, the wage of the person operating the machine — happens thousands of miles from the farm and after the commodity has changed hands. Green coffee is a raw input to an industry whose value is added at the end.

E   Growers are also poorly placed to capture more. The price of arabica is set on futures exchanges rather than negotiated farm by farm, so an individual producer is a price-taker with no leverage. Most holdings are under two hectares, which rules out both storage and the ability to wait for a better month. And because a coffee tree takes three to four years to bear, growers respond to high prices by planting, with the result that the crop arrives just as the price has fallen again.

What you should get from these paragraphs
  1. D opens with the one per cent figure — and then turns. "But the arithmetic is less mysterious than it looks" announces that the writer is about to explain the figure. So the figure is the starting point, not the subject.
  2. The rest of D answers the question "where does the money go?" Roasting, branding, rent, wages — all downstream, all after the farmer has sold. That is what the paragraph is about.
  3. E gives three separate reasons why growers cannot get more: prices set on exchanges, tiny holdings, and the planting lag. Three reasons, one topic sentence. Any heading naming only one of them is a detail.
  4. Read the futures sentence carefully. It says prices are set on exchanges rather than negotiated individually. It does not say prices are volatile. Those are different claims.

PATTERN 11Matching Headings
The paragraph whose point is in the last sentence
Technique

Most paragraphs announce their point in the first sentence. Expect roughly one per heading set that does not — where the writer agrees with something at length and then overturns it.

Passage — vertical farming

F   The promise of the vertical farm is easy to state and, on its own terms, real. What you should get from this paragraph
  1. About seventy per cent of the words describe advantages. If you judge by quantity alone you will choose the wrong heading.

  3. The turn has no "however". It is carried by repeating "survives inspection" with a negative: the arithmetic that does not survive inspection. Turns are often made of repeated words rather than linking words, which is why scanning for but is not enough.
  4. The last clause gives you the verdict outright: the energy cost is big enough "to undo the savings everywhere else". The paragraph exists to deliver that.

Passage — recycling glass

A   Glass is, in principle, the ideal material for a circular economy. It can be melted and reformed without any loss of quality, indefinitely, which is not true of paper or of most plastics. B   The obstacle is colour. Recycled glass, known in the trade as cullet, can only be melted into new containers of the same colour or darker. Countries that import more wine than they export accumulate green cullet they cannot use, and the surplus is crushed for road aggregate instead. A reprocessing plant in the north of England closed in 2019 for precisely this reason, unable to find buyers for the colour mix its catchment produced.

C   Where the colour problem is solved, the gains are substantial. Melting cullet requires a lower furnace temperature than raw sand, soda ash and limestone, and a batch containing half cullet cuts furnace energy by around fifteen per cent. This is a smaller saving than aluminium offers — remelting aluminium uses about five per cent of the energy of primary smelting — but glass is heavier, and the transport emissions saved by reprocessing locally are correspondingly larger.

D   Collection itself remains the weak link. Kerbside schemes reach most urban households but far fewer rural ones, and contamination is chronic: a single ceramic cup broken into a container of cullet can ruin an entire furnace batch, because ceramic does not melt at glass temperatures.

What you should get from these paragraphs
  1. There are two comparisons in this passage, not one. A compares glass with paper and plastics on quality loss. C compares glass with aluminium on energy. If a question asks for "a comparison", you must read what it is comparing them on.
  2. Paragraph B ends with a specific closure — a named place, a year, a business event. Questions asking for "an example of" nearly always point at this kind of sentence.
  3. Paragraph A contains "since the 1970s". That is a length of time. Notice that the seventy per cent figure sits in the same sentence and is about something else — adjacent numbers are a standing hazard in this question type.
  4. Paragraph D is about collection, but contains the ceramic sentence. This is the whole point of the pattern: the paragraph's topic and the information inside it are different things.

Passage — why we yawn

Yawning is universal among vertebrates and almost entirely unexplained. The oldest proposal, that a yawn corrects a shortage of oxygen, was tested in the 1980s and failed.

Attention has since turned to temperature. Andrew Gallup has argued that a yawn draws cool air across the sinuses and lowers the temperature of the brain; he reports that yawning becomes markedly less frequent when ambient temperatures exceed body temperature. Ronald Baenninger, whose earlier work on arousal Gallup cites approvingly, proposed something different: that yawning accompanies a change of state, marking the boundary between sleep and waking, and that its function is to raise alertness rather than to cool anything.

A third line of work concerns contagion. Molly Helt has shown that the tendency to yawn in response to another person's yawn appears in children at around four years of age and is weaker in children with autism, which she takes as evidence that contagious yawning depends on the same machinery as empathy. Passage — the Antikythera mechanism

When divers recovered a corroded lump of bronze from a Roman-era wreck in 1901, it was catalogued and ignored for half a century. Derek de Solla Price was the first to treat it seriously as a machine. Working from photographs in the 1950s, he counted the teeth on the visible gears and concluded that the device modelled the motions of the sun and moon — a claim widely disbelieved at the time, since no comparable gearing was known from antiquity for another thousand years. In 1971 Price obtained gamma-ray images that revealed a train of more than thirty gears and vindicated him. His reconstruction nevertheless contained errors, and he wrongly proposed a tooth count for one gear that later work corrected.

Michael Wright, a curator of mechanical engineering, spent two decades on the problem. He established that the mechanism included a lunar anomaly device — a pin-and-slot arrangement reproducing the moon's variable speed — which Price had not identified. Wright also argued, against the prevailing view, that the device had displayed the planets as well. Tony Freeth, leading a team that applied computed tomography in 2005, confirmed Wright's pin-and-slot reading and recovered inscriptions naming months in a Corinthian calendar, indicating an origin in the Greek west rather than in Rhodes as Price had assumed.

What you should get from this passage
  1. Price is mentioned five times, and each mention supports something different: he was first; he was disbelieved; he was vindicated; he made a tooth-count error; he assumed Rhodes. A reader who searches for "Price", reads the first sentence containing him and answers will get several of these wrong.

PATTERN 16Multiple Choice
The option that is true — but answers a different question
Technique

Wrong options in IELTS are rarely false. That is the thing to understand. They are usually true about something the question did not ask. So eliminating by asking "is this false?" eliminates nothing.

Cover the options. Find the relevant lines, answer the question in about six words of your own, then uncover the options and look for the one that matches what you already decided. This single habit removes most of the difficulty, because you stop asking "is this true?" and start asking "is this my answer?"

The four ways a wrong option is built: true but about something else; the passage's words with one element swapped; the right idea overstated; and plausible but never actually stated.

Passage — the waggle dance

A honeybee returning from a productive food source performs a figure-of-eight dance on the vertical surface of the comb. The straight run through the middle encodes two quantities: its duration is proportional to the distance of the source, and its angle from vertical corresponds to the angle between the source and the sun. Because the interior of the hive is dark, followers cannot see the dance; they read it by touch, and by the vibrations the dancer transmits through the comb. Quality is signalled separately and indirectly: a forager returning from a richer source dances for longer and repeats the circuit more often, which increases the number of bees recruited. Followers do not simply fly out along the vector described. Most make several orientation flights first, and a substantial proportion never locate the advertised source at all.

What you should get from this passage
  1. The straight run carries two separate pieces of information: duration = distance, angle = direction. Keep those apart. An option that swaps them is the classic one-element swap.
  2. Vibrations appear — but as the way followers perceive the dance, not as a signal of anything. This is the most useful sentence in the passage for understanding wrong options: everything in it is true, and it answers a different question from the one about quality.
  3. Quality is signalled by how long and how often the bee dances. Note the direction of the arrow: longer dance → more recruits. An option reversing that arrow is wrong.
  4. "Followers do not simply fly out along the vector" — a negative sentence, immediately before the sentence that answers a question. If you stop at the first half you get the opposite of the right answer.

PATTERN 17Multiple Choice
"Why does the writer mention…" — asking what a sentence is for
Technique

These questions are not asking what a sentence says. They are asking what job it does in the argument. The method is mechanical: read the sentence before it and the sentence after it, because a piece of evidence always serves the claim sitting next to it.

Why does the writer mention X?What X is there to do — look at the sentences on either side
What does the writer suggest?A conclusion the text supports across two or three sentences
What is the writer's attitude?Judgement words in the writer's own voice

The word suggest does not mean "guess". It means the answer is spread over several sentences rather than sitting in one. You must still be able to point at the evidence.

Passage — what GDP measures

Gross domestic product was designed in the 1930s to measure the output of an industrialising economy preparing for war, and at that job it remains excellent. Its awkwardness elsewhere is not a defect but a consequence of the design. A forest felled for timber enters the accounts as income; the disappearance of the forest enters nowhere. Unpaid care, which occupies more hours in most countries than paid employment does, is invisible. A country rebuilding after a flood records growth. None of this was concealed by the statisticians who built the system: Simon Kuznets, who did more than anyone to construct the American national accounts, told Congress in 1934 that the welfare of a nation could scarcely be inferred from a measure of national income. The difficulty is not that we were misled. It is that an instrument built for one purpose has been asked to serve as a general index of national success — and has obliged.

What you should get from this passage
  1. Find the claim, the evidence and the conclusion. The claim: "None of this was concealed by the statisticians." The evidence: Kuznets warned Congress in 1934. The conclusion: "The difficulty is not that we were misled." All three point the same way — the limitation was known from the start. That is what the Kuznets sentence is for.
  2. "Not a defect but a consequence of the design" is the spine of the whole paragraph, and the last sentence repeats it: an instrument built for one purpose asked to do another.
  3. Unpaid care is a real and memorable example — and it will attract an option suggesting we should start counting it. The passage never suggests that. Being mentioned is not being recommended.
  4. "None of this was concealed" is an acquittal, not an accusation. Read it carefully; an option will invert it.

PATTERN 18Multiple Choice — choose TWO
Five separate decisions, not "the best two"
Technique

Treat this as five independent true-or-false checks. Do not compare the options with each other.

  • It is worth two marks, one per correct letter, so getting one right is worth half.
  • Order does not matter.
  • Never select three. Over-selecting voids the whole question — you lose marks you had already earned.
  • If you can only verify one, guess the second. There is no penalty for a wrong letter, only for choosing too many.
Passage — desalination and its by-product

Reverse-osmosis desalination now supplies drinking water to more than three hundred million people. PATTERN 19Sentence Completion
Work out what kind of word the gap needs before you search
Technique

This is where people who understood everything still lose marks. The fix is procedural.

  1. Look at the words either side of the gap and decide the word type. After the you need a noun. After by in a passive you need a person or thing that acts. After more you need an adjective. After a preposition you need a noun, never a verb.
  2. Find it, and copy it exactly. Do not improve it.
  3. Count the words. Over the limit scores zero, however right the meaning.
  4. Read the finished sentence back. If it is not proper English, it is wrong.
Passage — the tunnelling shield

Marc Brunel patented the tunnelling shield in 1818 after studying the shipworm, a mollusc that bores through submerged oak. Brunel's machine worked on the same principle. A rectangular iron frame, divided into thirty-six cells, was pressed forward against the face of the excavation. Miners standing in the cells removed the boards in front of them one at a time, dug out a few inches of clay, and replaced them; behind the frame, bricklayers lined the completed section. What you should get from this passage
  1. Two numbers sit in the last sentence: five and eighteen. They count different things — floods and years. Whenever two numbers are close together, read what each one counts before you write either down.

  3. "Thirty-six cells" and "five occasions" — both are a number plus a noun. If the question already prints the noun, you only write the number.

PATTERN 20Summary Completion (no word list)
Copy the word. Do not improve it.
Technique

A summary is a compressed rewording of one section of the passage — usually a single continuous block, with the gaps in the same order as the text. That ordering is the most useful thing about this type: find the first gap, and the next one is a sentence or two further on.

Everything around the gap is reworded. The word in the gap is not. It is lifted exactly.

Passage — the shipping container

Malcom McLean, who ran a trucking company, was not interested in ships. He was interested in the hours his drivers spent idle at the dockside while cargo was loaded piece by piece, a process known as break-bulk handling. His answer, launched in 1956, was to lift whole truck trailers aboard a vessel; refining the idea, he settled on a standardised steel box that could pass between ship, rail and road without ever being opened. The dockworkers' unions resisted, correctly understanding that the box would eliminate most of their members' jobs: waterfront employment in New York fell by more than three-quarters within two decades. The principal saving, however, was not in labour but in time. A vessel that had once spent ten days in port could be turned around in under twenty-four hours.

What you should get from this passage
  1. "A process known as…" is a definition marker. Whenever you see known as, called, or that is, the term that follows is very likely to be tested.
  2. "More than three-quarters" — the passage uses words, not figures. So the answer must be words. Writing 75% means the same thing and scores zero, because the instruction says from the passage.
  3. "Not in labour but in time" — this is a trap shape. The wrong word (labour) is closer to the gap than the right one (time). In a not X but Y sentence, the answer is always Y.
  4. "Break-bulk" is hyphenated, so it counts as one word. Useful to know when the limit is tight.

The seven things that decide most of your marks
Carry these into the exam
  1. Read one sentence either side of the one you land on. The detail that changes the answer is usually in the next sentence. (Patterns 5, 8)
  2. Find the word in the question that has nothing to match it. If there is one, the answer is usually NOT GIVEN. If there is none, it is TRUE or FALSE. (Patterns 1, 3, 6)
  3. FALSE needs a clash you can point at. "It doesn't really say that" is NOT GIVEN, not FALSE. (Patterns 1, 4)
  4. Circle the amount words — all, most, some, only, never. Up the scale is FALSE; down the scale is TRUE; nothing on the scale is NOT GIVEN. (Patterns 2, 6)
  5. Check whose opinion it is before answering any Yes/No/Not Given question. (Pattern 7)
  6. In multiple choice, answer in your own words first. Wrong options are usually true about something else. (Patterns 16, 17)
  7. In completion, copy exactly, count the words, and never repeat a word already printed. (Patterns 19, 20, 22, 24)
